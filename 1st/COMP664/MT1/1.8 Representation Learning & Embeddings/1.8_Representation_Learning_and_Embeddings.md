# 1.8 Representation Learning & Embeddings

## From discrete symbols to learned vectors

An embedding maps a discrete item, such as a token, to a dense vector. An embedding table \(E\in\mathbb R^{|V|\times d}\) has one row per vocabulary item. Looking up the row for a token is equivalent to multiplying its one-hot row vector by \(E\). This mapping lets models learn relationships between tokens from data.

Tokenization determines the units represented by the table. A token can be a whole word or a subword. Keeping frequent words intact while splitting rare words into reusable pieces can limit vocabulary size and represent words that were rare or unseen as whole units.

## Learning from context

Distributional learning uses the contexts in which words appear as supervision. A small context window tends to emphasize nearby syntactic or functional relationships, while a larger window can emphasize topical associations. A window of radius two around a center position gives up to four context targets. For “the quick brown fox jumps,” the center word “brown” pairs with “the,” “quick,” “fox,” and “jumps.”

**Skip-gram** predicts context words from a center word. **Continuous bag-of-words (CBOW)** uses the surrounding context words to predict the center word. The text itself supplies targets for either objective.

## Skip-gram with full softmax

Use a center embedding \(u_c\) and a context embedding \(v_o\). Their dot product measures compatibility. For a center token \(c\) and a context token \(o\),

\[
P(o\mid c)=\frac{\exp(v_o^\top u_c)}{\sum_{w\in V}\exp(v_w^\top u_c)}.
\]

The training loss sums \(-\log P(o\mid c)\) over observed center–context pairs. For one pair,

\[
\nabla_{u_c}[-\log P(o\mid c)]
=\sum_{w\in V} P(w\mid c)v_w-v_o.
\]

The update moves the center vector toward the observed context vector relative to the context vectors the model currently expects. Full softmax requires summing over the vocabulary.

## Negative sampling

A binary objective can distinguish observed center–context pairs from sampled noise pairs. An observed pair such as “brown” and “fox” is a positive training example. Randomly sampled context words paired with the same center are treated as negatives. The score \(v_w^\top u_c\) goes through a sigmoid. With one observed context \(o\) and sampled negatives \(n_1,\ldots,n_K\), the loss is

\[
-\log\sigma(v_o^\top u_c)-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-v_{n_k}^\top u_c).
\]

The update uses the observed context and the sampled negatives, avoiding a full-vocabulary softmax.

## Interpreting and reusing embeddings

Words that occur in similar contexts can occupy nearby parts of the embedding space. Similarity can be compared with cosine similarity or inspected with a two-dimensional projection such as UMAP. A projection gives a partial view of the high-dimensional geometry. Some word relations also appear as vector offsets, as in the illustrative analogy \(\mathrm{king}-\mathrm{man}+\mathrm{woman}\approx\mathrm{queen}\).

Learned embedding tables can initialize another model. The downstream model can keep the vectors fixed or fine-tune them. A static embedding gives each token one vector across contexts, so a word with several senses has a single lookup representation.
