# 1.4 Backpropagation

## Chain rule and computational graphs

If \(z\) depends on \(x\) through intermediate values \(u_1,\ldots,u_m\), then

\[
\frac{dz}{dx}=\sum_{k=1}^m\frac{\partial z}{\partial u_k}\frac{du_k}{dx}.
\]

Multiply derivatives along each path and add the contributions of all paths. Backpropagation applies this rule from the loss toward the inputs and reuses gradients at shared intermediate nodes.

## Forward pass and notation

Use a column vector \(a^{(0)}=x\) for one example. For layer \(\ell\),

\[
z^{(\ell)}=W^{(\ell)}a^{(\ell-1)}+b^{(\ell)},\qquad
a^{(\ell)}=\phi_\ell(z^{(\ell)}).
\]

The preactivation \(z^{(\ell)}\) comes before the activation function. The postactivation \(a^{(\ell)}\) is passed to the next layer. With \(n_\ell\) units in layer \(\ell\), the weight matrix \(W^{(\ell)}\) is \(n_\ell\times n_{\ell-1}\). Save the values needed for local derivatives during the forward pass.

## Backward pass

Let \(\delta^{(\ell)}=\partial L/\partial z^{(\ell)}\). For hidden layers with elementwise activation,

\[
\delta^{(\ell)}
=\left((W^{(\ell+1)})^\top\delta^{(\ell+1)}\right)
 \odot \phi_\ell'(z^{(\ell)}).
\]

The transpose collects the weighted gradient contributions from the next layer. The elementwise product multiplies each by the local activation derivative. The parameter gradients are

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W^{(\ell)}}=
\delta^{(\ell)}(a^{(\ell-1)})^\top,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(\ell)}}=\delta^{(\ell)}.
\]

For weight \(W^{(\ell)}_{ij}\), the gradient is the preactivation gradient at unit \(i\) times the previous-layer activation at unit \(j\). The weight gradient is an outer product with the same shape as \(W^{(\ell)}\). Sum or average per-example contributions according to the definition of the batch loss. The optimizer uses these gradients to update parameters.

## Output-layer example

With softmax probabilities \(p_i\), logits \(s_j\), and one-hot targets \(y_i\),

\[
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial s_j}=p_i(\mathbf1_{i=j}-p_j),\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_i}=-\frac{y_i}{p_i}.
\]

Summing the product over \(i\) gives \(\partial L/\partial s_j=p_j-y_j\). With binary sigmoid and cross-entropy, the same prediction-minus-target form holds for the single logit.

For a scalar hidden unit \(h=\sigma(w_1x)\), output logit \(s=w_2h\), and prediction \(p=\sigma(s)\), binary cross-entropy gives

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2}=(p-y)h,\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}=(p-y)w_2h(1-h)x.
\]

## Gradient propagation in deep networks

Repeated chain-rule multiplication can make gradients small or large as they travel through many layers. Sigmoid's derivative is at most \(1/4\) and becomes smaller in its saturated regions. This can contribute to vanishing gradients in deep sigmoid networks. Weight matrices also affect the size of the gradient at each step.
